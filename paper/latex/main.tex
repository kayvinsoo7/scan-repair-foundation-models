\begin{abstract}
Tabular foundation models have recently become competitive with tuned tree-based predictors. They
use labeled examples as context to make predictions without task-specific training. Increasing this
context can improve predictive performance, but may also raise computational costs. An alternative is
to improve predictions made with a smaller context by correcting errors concentrated in particular
subgroups. This paper proposes Structured Scan Repair (SSR), a post-hoc method that uses a one-time
repair to recover some of the predictive benefit of a larger context, without requiring that larger
context at every prediction. SSR searches for such subgroups using rules on the original features,
validates candidate corrections on held-out data, and applies a few interpretable log-odds
adjustments or abstains, leaving the underlying model unchanged. We show that SSR enables TabPFN-2.5
with a 1{,}000-row context to achieve predictive performance comparable to the uncorrected model
using about 2{,}200 and 4{,}200 context rows on Credit Default and a synthetic planted-subgroup
benchmark, respectively, at an estimated \CrEqScanCpu--\PlEqScanCpu$\times$ lower recurring CPU
inference cost. Across tuned XGBoost, CatBoost, TabPFN-2.5, and TabICLv2, SSR reduces planted
subgroup residual bias by \DBiasRedLo--\DBiasRedHi\% when sufficient discovery data are available.
Compiled, batched, and parallel scoring accelerates repair fitting by \SpBatMin--\SpBatMax$\times$
on CPU without changing the selected corrections. The inference-cost advantage is hardware-dependent,
falling to \IclCrEqScanGpu--\IclPlEqScanGpu$\times$ for TabICLv2 on a T4 GPU. These findings
highlight the conditions under which targeted subgroup repair can offer a computationally efficient
alternative to increasing foundation-model context.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================
Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2023tabpfn,hollmann2025nature,grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}
and TabICL~\cite{qu2025tabicl,qu2026tabiclv2} have recently become competitive with tuned
gradient-boosted trees~\cite{erickson2025tabarena,chen2016xgboost,prokhorenkova2018catboost}. Rather
than training a separate model for each dataset, they predict new observations using labeled examples
supplied as context. Context is the set of labeled rows provided at prediction time, and context size
is the number of those rows. That convenience moves cost from training to inference. A boosted
ensemble is trained once and scored cheaply, whereas an in-context model processes its context to make
predictions. A larger context can improve predictive performance, but the additional inference cost
recurs. On a two-core CPU, for example, TabPFN-2.5 takes \CpuSecOne{} seconds to score 1{,}000
observations with a 1{,}000-row context, compared with \CpuSecFull{} seconds using 15{,}000 rows. For
applications using foundation models, context size must be chosen with CPU, memory, and latency
constraints in mind.

Rather than always adding context, we could examine the errors left by a smaller-context model. If
some are concentrated in subgroups defined by combinations of features, we may be able to correct
them without repeatedly processing a larger context. We ask:
{\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}\setlength{\partopsep}{0pt}\begin{quote}
\itshape When can a one-time subgroup repair recover part of the predictive benefit of a
larger context at a fraction of its recurring cost?
\end{quote}}
Such a repair needs labeled observations for discovery and validation, and those observations could
instead be added to the foundation model's context. We compare repair and additional context as uses
of inference computation, not as equivalent uses of labeled data. In our experiments, adding those
observations directly to the context gives better predictions when computation is unrestricted.

Existing methods address prediction errors in different ways. Global recalibration methods, including
temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration}, Platt scaling~\cite{platt1999}, beta
calibration~\cite{kull2017beta}, isotonic regression~\cite{zadrozny2002}, and histogram
binning~\cite{zadrozny2001}, adjust prediction scores without explicitly targeting subgroups defined
by the original features. Multicalibration~\cite{hebert2018multicalibration} and
multiaccuracy~\cite{kim2019multiaccuracy} extend correction to pre-specified classes of subgroups or
auditing functions, while subgroup recalibration has also been applied to clinical risk
models~\cite{barda2021subpopulation}. Conventional multicalibration algorithms often discretize
prediction scores and adjust them repeatedly, introducing rounding errors and additional tuning
choices. Jin et al.~\cite{jin2025discretization} avoid this discretization by optimizing a loss over
shallow tree ensembles. MCGrad~\cite{tax2026mcgrad} learns boosted-tree corrections without manually
specifying groups and uses validation-based early stopping to guard against overfitting. Its learned
correction function and global logit rescaling can also alter predictions outside the regions being
corrected, making individual changes harder to isolate. Other methods focus on discovering errors:
slice-finding methods~\cite{chung2019slicefinder,sagadeeva2021sliceline} identify high-loss subsets,
while predictive-bias scanning~\cite{zhang2016bias}, based on subset scanning~\cite{neill2012fast},
detects miscalibrated subgroups and demonstrates refitting with the detected subgroup terms, and
more recent work~\cite{boxer2026intersectional} audits intersectional prediction errors. In our setting,
we restrict correction to a few individually validated subgroup rules, leaving other predictions
unaffected, and compare the predictive benefit with the recurring cost of a larger context.

We propose Structured Scan Repair (SSR), a post-hoc method that identifies subgroups with systematic
prediction errors and selectively corrects them. SSR searches over rules defined on the original
features, scoring each by the maximum Bernoulli log-likelihood gain from a common log-odds
adjustment~\cite{zhang2016bias}. Candidate corrections are evaluated on selection data held out from
discovery. SSR selects corrections over successive rounds and abstains if none qualify. The
base model and its context remain unchanged, while inference adds only a few rule comparisons.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A validated, model-agnostic repair method} (Secs.~\ref{sec:method},~\ref{sec:general}).
With 9{,}600 discovery rows, SSR reduces planted subgroup residual bias by
\DBiasRedLo--\DBiasRedHi\% across tuned XGBoost, CatBoost, TabPFN-2.5 and TabICLv2, with near-zero
average change in test log-loss when no subgroup effect is planted.
\item \textbf{A context--compute trade-off for tabular foundation models} (Sec.~\ref{sec:ladder}).
SSR enables TabPFN-2.5 with a 1{,}000-row context to achieve predictive performance comparable to the
uncorrected model using approximately 2{,}200 and 4{,}200 context rows on Credit Default and a
synthetic planted-subgroup benchmark, respectively, at an estimated
\CrEqScanCpu--\PlEqScanCpu$\times$ lower recurring CPU inference cost. The advantage depends on
hardware and falls to \IclCrEqScanGpu--\IclPlEqScanGpu$\times$ for TabICLv2 on a T4 GPU.
\item \textbf{Evidence that repair and additional context address overlapping errors}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:attr}). Observations flagged by SSR account for \PlConcLo--\PlConcHi$\times$ their
expected share of the improvement delivered by full context on the planted benchmark
(\CrConcLo--\CrConcHi$\times$ on Credit Default). At smaller contexts, SSR moves their predictions in
the same direction as the full-context model in \CrSignLo--\CrSignHi\% of cases, compared with
\PlattSignLo--\PlattSignHi\% for Platt scaling.
\item \textbf{Exact CPU acceleration of SSR fitting} (Sec.~\ref{sec:systems}). Compiled, batched and
parallel candidate scoring accelerates fitting by \SpBatMin--\SpBatMax$\times$ without changing the
selected rules or fitted shifts. A repair fits in seconds on a single CPU core.
\end{itemize}

\section{Structured Scan Repair}\label{sec:method}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{Setting.} A fitted probabilistic classifier, the base model, outputs a logit $F(x)$ and probability
$p(x)=\sigma(F(x))$. Once repair begins, the base model, including the context of an in-context model,
is never updated: only additive subgroup shifts are learned. Labeled observations not used to construct the base predictor are partitioned
into \emph{discovery}, \emph{selection} and \emph{test} sets. A subgroup $S$ is an interpretable
conjunction of literals on the original features (numeric thresholds or categorical subsets). After
$T$ accepted corrections, the repaired predictor is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:repair}
F_R(x) = F(x) + \textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta_t\, \mathbf{1}[x\in S_t].
\end{equation}

\textbf{Score.} For a candidate subgroup $S$, SSR asks how much the Bernoulli
log-likelihood would improve if every member of $S$ received the same log-odds shift $\delta$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
\Delta(S,\delta) = \sum_{i\in S}\Big[y_i\delta - \log\big(1-p_i+e^{\delta}p_i\big)\Big].
\end{equation}
The objective is concave in $\delta$, so Newton iteration gives
$\Delta^\star(S)=\max_\delta\Delta(S,\delta)$. At the optimum,
$\sum_{i\in S}[y_i-\sigma(F_i+\delta)]=0$, so the mean repaired probability within $S$ matches
its observed event rate. The statistic is the predictive-bias scan score~\cite{zhang2016bias} with $q=e^{\delta}$; here
$\hat\delta$ is applied directly as an offset to the base model's logit, and $\Delta^\star(S)$ is exactly
the reduction in total log-loss on $S$.

\textbf{Search.} Exhaustively enumerating multi-feature conjunctions is infeasible, so rules grow
one literal at a time in a diversity-aware beam (width 24, maximum depth 5). For a numeric feature
under a parent rule, candidate thresholds are first ranked by the second-order proxy
$\tfrac12 G^2/H$, where $G$ and $H$ are prefix sums of $p_i-y_i$ and $p_i(1-p_i)$ over the sorted
feature. Only a shortlist is evaluated using the exact likelihood gain in~\eqref{eq:gain}.
Categorical levels are ordered so that only prefix subsets need be considered.

\textbf{Validation and abstention.} Discovery proposes candidate rules; selection determines
whether any is sufficiently stable to apply. Each round passes at most five candidates to
selection: the four highest-scoring distinct beam rules and one rule from a fixed five-bin subset
scan. Each candidate is independently refitted on two
stratified halves $V_1,V_2$ of the selection set. Candidates are retained only when they improve
likelihood on both halves and the fitted shifts have the same sign. Among these, SSR selects
the rule maximizing the smaller per-row gain across the two halves. A nominal one-degree-of-freedom
likelihood-ratio test for $\delta=0$ is then applied to this single rule on the complete selection
set at $\alpha=0.05$, as in the replication stage of a two-stage association study~\cite{kraft2009replication}. If it fails, the round terminates; the algorithm does not fall back to a
runner-up. On real data, accepted rules are additionally audited on the test set: subgroup residual
must shrink, removing the rule must worsen test log-loss, and the rule must beat the 95th percentile
of 500 score-matched placebo subgroups. This audit is diagnostic and does not alter the fitted
repair.

\textbf{Iterative repair.} SSR performs at most four rounds. After an accepted rule is
added, all accepted shifts are jointly refitted on selection data with $F$ as a fixed offset and an
$\ell_2$ coefficient penalty of $10^{-3}$. The updated residuals are then scanned again. The process
stops at the first round with no stable candidate or when the selected rule fails the whole-selection
test.

\textbf{Two-stage variant.} R-SSR (recalibrated SSR) fits Platt scaling on discovery data and then applies
SSR to the recalibrated logits; the selection set remains reserved for validating
candidate subgroup corrections. This variant removes a global calibration component before searching
for subgroup error.

\textbf{What a correction does.} Each accepted rule adds a constant log-odds shift to the
observations it covers; for a single rule this multiplies their odds by $e^{\delta}$ and preserves
their ranking. Where rules overlap, shifts add in log-odds; observations outside all accepted rules
are unchanged. A fitted repair is thus a small collection of explicit
subgroup rules and one scalar shift per rule rather than a second high-capacity predictor.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Structured Scan Repair (SSR)}
\label{alg:ssr}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Base-model logit $F$; discovery $D$; selection $V=V_1\cup V_2$ (stratified halves);
$T=4$; beam width 24; depth $\leq5$; $\alpha=0.05$; $\ell_2$ penalty $\lambda=10^{-3}$
\Ensure Repaired logit $F_R$ and rule set $\mathcal R$ (possibly empty)
\State $\mathcal R\gets\emptyset$; $F_R\gets F$
\For{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
    \State $\mathcal C\gets$ up to 5 rules from scans of $D$, offset $F_R$
    \State for each $S\in\mathcal C$: $(g_h,\hat\delta_h)\gets$ maximize~\eqref{eq:gain} on $V_h$, $h=1,2$
    \State $\mathcal A\gets\{S\in\mathcal C:\ g_1,g_2>0,\ \operatorname{sign}\hat\delta_1=\operatorname{sign}\hat\delta_2\}$
    \If{$\mathcal A=\emptyset$}
        \State \textbf{break} \Comment{abstain from further repair}
    \EndIf
    \State $S_t\gets\arg\max_{S\in\mathcal A}\min_h g_h(S)/|V_h|$ \Comment{most stable}
    \State $p_{\mathrm{LR}}\gets$ nominal 1-df LR $p$-value for $S_t$ on all of $V$
    \If{$p_{\mathrm{LR}}\ge\alpha$}
        \State \textbf{break} \Comment{reject; no runner-up fallback}
    \EndIf
    \State $\mathcal R\gets\mathcal R\cup\{S_t\}$
    \State $\boldsymbol\delta\gets\arg\max\,\ell_V\big(F+\sum_{j\le t}\delta_j\mathbf{1}[x\in S_j]\big)
    -\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol\delta\|^2$
    \State $F_R(x)\gets F(x)+\sum_{j\le t}\delta_j\mathbf{1}[x\in S_j]$
\EndFor
\State \Return $F_R,\mathcal R$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{Data and splits.} The controlled synthetic benchmark has $n=12{,}000$ observations and
$p=30$ independent features $X_j\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. The planted subgroup is
$S^*=\{X_1>c,\ldots,X_5>c\}$ with $c=1-0.08^{1/5}\approx0.397$ (8\% expected support), and a background
signal uses the next six features, $X_{\mathrm{bg}}=(X_6,\ldots,X_{11})$. Outcomes are Bernoulli with
\begin{equation*}
\Pr(Y{=}1\mid X)=\sigma\big(b_0+(2X_{\mathrm{bg}}-1)^\top\beta+\log(\theta)\,\mathbf{1}[X\in S^*]\big),
\end{equation*}
with $\beta=(1,-0.8,0.7,-0.6,0.5,-0.4)$, $\theta=8$ in the planted condition and $\theta=1$ in the
no-planted-effect control (the background signal remains; only the conjunction effect is absent). For each
generated dataset, $b_0$ is solved after including the subgroup term so that the mean event probability
over the sample is 10\%. Both conditions use
6{,}000 observations for base-model construction and 2{,}400/1{,}800/1{,}800 for
 discovery/selection/test. \emph{Credit Default}~\cite{yeh2009credit} contains 30{,}000 clients and
23 predictors (3 categorical); 15{,}000 observations construct the base model and
6{,}000/4{,}500/4{,}500 form discovery/selection/test. We use ten seeds per setting; within each
seed, every base model and correction uses the same partition. Each synthetic seed is a fresh draw
from the generator; on Credit the 4{,}500 test rows are fixed and the seeds re-partition the
remaining rows.

\textbf{Base models.} XGBoost and CatBoost are tuned once per dataset by
5-fold cross-validated random search over 25 configurations (log-loss, early stopping) on the first
seed's model-building partition; the selected hyperparameters are then refitted on each seed's
model-building partition. Tuning never uses test rows. TabPFN-2.5~\cite{grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}
(\texttt{tabpfn} 9.1.0) and TabICLv2~\cite{qu2026tabiclv2} (\texttt{tabicl} 2.2.0) use their
released default configurations and receive the complete model-building split as labeled context.
Each family thus receives a standard, well-tuned configuration, though not an exhaustive search.
Once base prediction is complete, every correction method treats all models identically: it receives
the original features, labels and that model's logits on the common correction partitions.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Credit Default has no missing values. Its three categorical variables are
integer-coded using the categories seen in the model-building rows. XGBoost receives these codes as
numeric features; CatBoost, TabPFN-2.5 and TabICLv2 receive them marked as categorical. SSR rules
are reported in the original named feature space. No preprocessing step uses test labels.

\textbf{Post-hoc comparators.} Every correction receives the same correction-data budget; its own
fitting and validation use the common discovery and selection partitions. We evaluate temperature
scaling, Platt scaling, beta calibration, isotonic regression, 15-bin equal-mass histogram binning,
a single residual tree (maximum depth 3, selected on the selection data), a boosted residual
corrector (a baseline using the residual-boosting update underlying methods such as
MCGrad~\cite{tax2026mcgrad}, not a full MCGrad implementation: gradient-boosted trees fitted from the base-model logit and
early-stopped on selection; median 63--161 leaves per setting), SSR, and R-SSR. Table~\ref{tab:bench} reports the full cross-model battery.

\textbf{Timing.} Inference time is measured on a 2-core CPU and a T4 GPU. CPU timing uses the released
TabPFN-2.5 architecture (10.7M parameters) with randomly initialized weights, as weight values do not
change CPU time (checked by rescaling all weights); all predictive results, and GPU timing, use the released weights.

\textbf{Evaluation and statistical analysis.} The primary metric is test log-loss (LL), reported
absolutely and as improvement over the corresponding base model. For the planted condition
we additionally report absolute residual bias on $S^*$, the \emph{oracle} gain obtained by applying
the optimal one-rule shift to the known subgroup, and Jaccard overlap between the detected and true
subgroups. On Credit we report the held-out audit pass rate; harm counts seeds whose
correction increases test log-loss. Across the ten matched seeds, figures report paired 95\%
confidence intervals where shown, and starred table entries use two-sided paired $t$-tests at
$p<0.05$. These $p$-values are descriptive and are not multiplicity-adjusted.

\section{When Should We Repair Rather Than Buy More Context?}\label{sec:ladder}
%=====================================================================
For an in-context model, increasing context can improve predictive performance while raising
recurring inference cost (Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}); a T4 GPU is faster than the CPU but scales similarly
(\GpuSecOne\,s vs.\ \GpuSecFull\,s per 1{,}000 rows). We run TabPFN-2.5 on a \emph{ladder} of nested contexts: for each seed,
the model-building split is shuffled once and context $k$ is its first $k$ rows,
$k\in\{500,1\text{k},1.5\text{k},2\text{k},3\text{k},4\text{k},6\text{k},8\text{k},10\text{k}\}$
and full. Logits are saved once per (dataset, seed, $k$); every correction is then fitted on CPU
using the standard correction splits of Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}.

\subsection{Context reduces the repair opportunity}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.84\textwidth]{fig_gain_ladder.pdf}
\caption{Held-out log-loss improvement over uncorrected TabPFN-2.5 at the same context (mean and paired
95\% CI over 10 seeds; positive is better). The panels show the planted subgroup, the matched
no-planted-effect control, and Credit Default. Global Platt recalibration gives modest gains, whereas
local or flexible corrections can give larger gains at small contexts. SSR contracts as

\label{fig:gain}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:gain} shows the main corrections across context. On the planted problem,
SSR improves test log-loss by \PlOneScan$\times10^{-3}$ $[\PlOneScanLo,\PlOneScanHi]$ at 1{,}000
context rows, compared with \PlOnePlatt{} for Platt, and captures
\PlOracleShareLo--\PlOracleShareHi\% of the oracle one-rule gain at 500--1{,}000 rows. From 1{,}000
rows onward it matches or exceeds the boosted residual corrector on this structured error. On Credit,
SSR gains \CrFiveScan{} at 500 rows and \CrOneScan{} $[\CrOneScanLo,\CrOneScanHi]$ at 1{,}000 rows,
although the boosted residual corrector gives the largest raw log-loss improvement throughout the
ladder.

The decline in SSR gain at larger contexts reflects a shrinking target rather than simply poorer
search. On the planted benchmark, the base model's subgroup bias falls from \PlFiveResFrozen{} at 500 context
rows to \PlFullResFrozen{} at full context, while the oracle one-rule gain falls from
\PlFiveOracle{} to \PlFullOracle$\times10^{-3}$. SSR captures 40--73\% of this
remaining one-rule opportunity across the ladder (Table~\ref{tab:diag}). At full context its mean
gain is only \PlFullScan$\times10^{-3}$ on the planted problem and \CrFullScan$\times10^{-3}$ on Credit.

\subsection{SSR targets many of the errors that more context later reduces}\label{sec:attr}

Similar aggregate log-loss does not by itself show that repair and additional context address the
same errors. We therefore compare TabPFN at each context $k$ with the corresponding full-context
predictor row by row (Fig.~\ref{fig:attr}). Observations flagged by SSR receive
\PlConcLo--\PlConcHi$\times$ their expected share of the improvement delivered by full context on
the planted problem, and \CrConcLo--\CrConcHi$\times$ on Credit for $k\le4$k; random rows would
receive $1\times$. At 500--1{,}000 context rows, SSR moves corrected logits in the same direction
as the full-context model in \PlSignLo--\PlSignHi\% of planted cases and
\CrSignLo--\CrSignHi\% of Credit cases. Platt's global transformation agrees in only
\PlattSignLo--\PlattSignHi\% of rows, about the chance level. This alignment weakens as
$k$ approaches full context and little incremental improvement remains. At constrained
contexts, SSR flags many of the observations that additional context later improves.

\textbf{Resource accounting.} A repaired 1{,}000-row TabPFN uses held-out labels once, whereas a
larger-context TabPFN pays for its extra rows on every prediction, so the comparison below is one of
deployment compute, not of labels. If all
held-out rows are instead placed into context (\PlLabCtx{} rows on the planted data, \CrLabCtx{} on
Credit), test log-loss is lower (about \PlLabLL{} and \CrLabLL{}) but costs
\PlLabCpu$\times$ and \CrLabCpu$\times$ more CPU inference per prediction than the 1{,}000-row
model (\PlLabGpu$\times$ and \CrLabGpu$\times$ on the T4). Repair does not dominate context when
compute is free.

Because a fitted subgroup repair adds only a few rule comparisons at inference, its recurring cost
is essentially that of the underlying small-context TabPFN. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}
compares predictive loss with recurring prediction cost. At a 1{,}000-row context, SSR
reaches a Credit test loss matched by the uncorrected model only at \CrEqScan{} context rows, and a
planted-subgroup loss matched at \PlEqScan{} rows. Equivalent contexts and costs are interpolated between measured ladder
points. Achieving the same loss through additional
context makes each CPU prediction \CrEqScanCpu$\times$ and \PlEqScanCpu$\times$ more expensive,
respectively (\CrEqScanGpu$\times$ and \PlEqScanGpu$\times$ on the T4). Platt scaling corresponds to an
equivalent-context multiplier of only \PlEqPlattX--\CrEqPlattX$\times$. The boosted residual corrector matches a larger
context than SSR on Credit (\CrEqXgb{} rows versus \CrEqScan{}) but a smaller one on the planted
problem (\PlEqXgb{} versus \PlEqScan{}).

On Credit, the foundation models lead the tuned trees only at full context (test LL \BPfnCrLL{}
against \BXgbCrLL, a margin comparable to the \BPfnCrPlatt$\times10^{-3}$ that Platt scaling adds);
at 1{,}000 rows, even after repair (\CrScanOneLL), TabPFN-2.5 trails the tuned XGBoost by about
$8\times10^{-3}$. On the planted benchmark, where the residual is localized, the repaired 1{,}000-row
model roughly matches it (\PlScanOneLL{} against \BXgbPlLL). Repair does not make a small-context
foundation model beat a tuned tree in general; it narrows the gap to a larger context for a
deployment that has already chosen the foundation model.

\textbf{A second foundation model.} TabICLv2 on the same ladder (same nested contexts, splits and
corrections) shows a similar predictive-performance pattern. At 1{,}000 rows SSR matches the uncorrected model at
\IclPlEqScan{} rows on the planted subgroup and \IclCrEqScan{} on Credit (R-SSR:
\IclPlEqPS{} and \IclCrEqPS), and the rows it flags again receive \IclConcLo--\IclConcHi$\times$ their
share of the full-context improvement, moved in its direction in \IclSignLo--\IclSignHi\% of cases.
Its cost scaling differs. On the CPU, TabICLv2's cost per prediction grows almost linearly with context
(\IclCpuSecOne\,s per 1{,}000 rows at 1{,}000 rows, \IclCpuSecFour\,s at 4{,}000), so matching the
repair with more context costs \IclCrEqScanCpu--\IclPlEqScanCpu$\times$ more. On the T4 it barely grows
(\IclGpuSecOne\,s to \IclGpuSecFour\,s), and the saving shrinks to \IclCrEqScanGpu--\IclPlEqScanGpu$\times$.
Whether repair is cheaper than context depends on how the model's inference cost scales with
context on the hardware that serves it.

\subsection{Abstention as the model strengthens}
As confirmed subgroup error disappears, a selective repair should intervene less. On
Credit, the fraction of seeds with an accepted SSR rule falls from \CrFiveInterv{} at 500 rows to
\CrFullInterv{} at full context, while the fraction of accepted rules passing the audit falls from
\CrFiveAudit{} to \CrFullAudit. In the no-planted-effect condition, mean SSR gain approaches zero
as context grows, although the nominal acceptance criterion still admits some spurious rules: from
2{,}000 rows onward, \NuHarmLo--\NuHarmHi{} of 10 seeds are made slightly worse, with a
mean change of $\NuFullScan\times10^{-3}$ at full context. In these foundation-model settings,
R-SSR has a positive mean at every context (\NuPSMin{} to \NuPSMax), consistent with
it removing broad miscalibration before searching for subgroup error. The two-stage variant suits
settings where global and local calibration errors coexist; it is not uniformly better than SSR.

\section{Structured Repair Across Model Families}\label{sec:general}
%=====================================================================
We now ask whether the targeted error, and its repair, are specific to in-context models.

\subsection{All four base models leave a common subgroup error}
Table~\ref{tab:bench} compares the four base models under the standard correction-data
budget. On the planted benchmark their overall test log-loss is similar, yet all four retain
substantial error on the known subgroup: absolute residual bias is \BBiasLo--\BBiasHi, and
correcting the true subgroup with its optimal one-rule shift would improve test log-loss by
\BOracleLo--\BOracleHi$\times10^{-3}$. Global recalibration changes this subgroup error only
modestly; Platt, beta and isotonic reduce subgroup bias by \BCalRedLo--\BCalRedHi\%. With only 2{,}400 discovery
observations, however, SSR does not recover the subgroup reliably: it accepts a rule in
\BXgbPlRep{} XGBoost, \BCatPlRep{} CatBoost, \BIclPlRep{} TabICLv2 and \BPfnPlRep{} TabPFN-2.5
runs. Consequently, its mean gains at this discovery size are small, whereas R-SSR gives
the largest mean improvement on the planted condition for all four base models.

\subsection{Repairability is not the same as detectability}
The weak 2{,}400-row result does not mean that the tree models have absorbed the planted
structure. The true subgroup remains worth a similar discovery-set likelihood gain under all four
models (13--17 nats on average). The difficulty is statistical: identifying a five-feature
conjunction among 30 variables from 2{,}400 observations leaves many chance patterns with competitive
scores. We therefore repeat the experiment on a separate $n=19{,}200$ draw and, within that draw,
hold the base models, selection set and test set fixed while varying only SSR's discovery
sample from 2{,}400 to 9{,}600 observations (Table~\ref{tab:disc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:disc}).

With 9{,}600 discovery observations, the earlier dependence on the base model largely disappears.
SSR accepts a repair in \DRepLo--10 of 10 runs for every model, recovers
\DShareLo--\DShareHi\% of the best available one-rule gain, and identifies rules with Jaccard
\DJacLo--\DJacHi{} overlap with the true subgroup. The correction reduces absolute subgroup bias
from \DBiasFLo--\DBiasFHi{} to \DBiasSLo--\DBiasSHi, a \DBiasRedLo--\DBiasRedHi\% reduction.
In the matched no-planted-effect control, increasing discovery data does not produce a corresponding
increase in harm: mean SSR gain remains within $\pm$\DNullAbsMax$\times10^{-3}$, with at most
\DNullHarmMax{} harmful seeds. In this controlled setting, the main limitation at the smaller
discovery size is detectability: the repairable error is present but hard to find.

\subsection{When little subgroup error remains, SSR mostly abstains}
At full model-building context, Credit Default presents a different regime. The two foundation models have the lowest test log-loss
(LL \BPfnCrLL--\BIclCrLL; AUROC \BPfnCrAUC--\BIclCrAUC), compared with LL
\BXgbCrLL--\BCatCrLL{} for the tuned trees. Across all four models, SSR finds little
validated subgroup error: it intervenes in only \BCatCrRep--\BPfnCrRep{} runs and changes mean
test log-loss by at most $0.3\times10^{-3}$. The foundation models instead retain modest global
miscalibration, for which Platt and beta improve log-loss by about
$1.3$--$1.5\times10^{-3}$; corresponding gains for the tuned trees are at most
$0.2\times10^{-3}$.

The no-planted-effect control shows the same distinction: global recalibration still helps the
foundation models, whereas SSR has near-zero average effect. Across the full correction battery,
isotonic regression and histogram binning frequently worsen test log-loss, while the boosted
residual corrector improves it in all 12 model--dataset settings and is strongest among the alternatives for several
Credit and control settings. That gain comes from a much higher-capacity correction,
typically tens to hundreds of leaves, whereas SSR seeks a few named subgroup shifts, each validated
on held-out data, and can return the model unchanged. When a strong base model leaves little confirmed
subgroup error, abstaining is the intended outcome.

\section{Exact CPU Acceleration of Structured Scan Repair}\label{sec:systems}
%=====================================================================
Profiling the reference implementation on the planted benchmark shows that approximately
\CostScorerShare\% of fit time is spent in the exact scorer~\eqref{eq:gain}, which is invoked
roughly 65{,}000 times for a standard 2{,}400-row, 30-feature discovery set. We optimize
this bottleneck without changing any of SSR's statistical decisions. First, a Numba
implementation~\cite{lam2015numba} fuses each Newton solve into one compiled loop while preserving
its iteration, clipping and stopping rules. Second, shortlisted thresholds for a
(feature, parent-rule) pair are scored in one compiled batch using lookup tables rather than
repeated set-membership operations. Third, independent candidates are evaluated in parallel while
retaining each candidate's original summation order.

These changes leave candidate scores, selected rules and fitted shifts unchanged in all
\SpConfigs{} synthetic configurations and on Credit Default. At the default problem size they
reduce one repair fit from \SpOrigDefault\,s to \SpBatDefault\,s, a \SpBatX$\times$ speed-up, and
give \SpBatMin--\SpBatMax$\times$ acceleration across the tested discovery sizes on a 2-core CPU
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}). Across the TabPFN context ladder, median fitting time is \ScanSecMed\,s on
one CPU core. Faster search also reduces the cost of calibrating the acceptance threshold through hundreds of
repeated null scans (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\columnwidth]{fig_cost.pdf}
\caption{Exact CPU acceleration of one SSR fit as discovery size grows (30 features,
2-core CPU). The compiled scorer and the batched+parallel implementation preserve the reference
arithmetic and select identical rules; the latter gives 3.9--6.2$\times$ speed-up across the tested
sizes.}
\label{fig:cost}

\textbf{Towards an accelerator kernel.} The acceleration above is exact. As a separate exploratory
direction, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} can be approximated for candidate screening by summarizing subgroup
logits into $B=64$ quantile bins, reducing each Newton solve to 64 aggregated values. On
\FidHistN{} random conjunctions this approximation selects the same best candidate and top 50 as
exact scoring, with maximum gain error \FidHistErr\,nats. With feature-wise prefix sums, threshold
screening becomes a dense (feature$\times$threshold$\times$bin) tensor operation suited to GPUs or
other accelerators; shortlisted candidates would still be re-scored exactly before selection.

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Outlook}\label{sec:disc}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{When repair pays.} When residual error is concentrated in identifiable subgroups, one-time
repair recovers part of the benefit of a larger context at lower recurring cost (an estimated
\CrEqScanCpu--\PlEqScanCpu$\times$ for TabPFN-2.5 on CPU; less for TabICLv2 on the T4). In the planted benchmark, tuned trees and foundation models leave a
comparable subgroup error. Global recalibration changes that error only modestly, whereas SSR
removes most of it once enough discovery data are available. Credit shows the complementary regime: when a
strong base model leaves little validated subgroup error, SSR mostly abstains, while global
recalibration or a higher-capacity residual learner may be more useful.

No single correction is best across these regimes. Global calibration suits broad residual error; a flexible boosted
residual learner can maximize predictive gain when additional correction complexity is acceptable;
SSR is designed for sparse, interpretable subgroup errors that can be independently
validated and rejected when evidence is weak. Unlike multicalibration boosting~\cite{tax2026mcgrad},
SSR changes only rows inside accepted rules, which keeps each adjustment inspectable; in exchange, it
offers no guarantee over a class of groups.

\textbf{Limitations.} The study uses one synthetic generator, one real dataset and two in-context
models; broader validation is needed. Structured subgroup discovery is data-hungry
(Sec.~\ref{sec:general}). The acceptance test is nominal because the selection data
also choose among up to five candidates; a simulation for TabPFN-2.5 with 200 null scans per setting
puts its false-acceptance rate at about 13\%. A simulation-calibrated threshold ($2\Delta\approx5.5$ rather than 3.84; 2{,}000 rows and full
context) changes planted gains by at most $0.4\times10^{-3}$ and moves the full-context
no-planted-effect change from $-0.8$ to $-0.1\times10^{-3}$.

\textbf{Next steps and open questions.} The results motivate four extensions: (a) an
accelerator-oriented candidate-scoring kernel; (b) context-level repair; (c) repair under temporal
shift, compared with refreshing the context; and (d) structured correction inside model training,
a distinct problem because it is no longer post hoc. Whether repair beats more context depends on how much
structured error remains and how inference cost grows with context on the serving hardware.

\section*{Reproducibility}
Base-model logits are saved once for every dataset, seed and context, and all corrections, tables
and figures are regenerated from those saved predictions on CPU. Foundation-model runs
use a T4 GPU; repeating TabICLv2 on an A100 changes base-model test log-loss by at most
$7\times10^{-5}$ per run and no accept/abstain decision. The Credit Default
analysis uses the original UCI data. Code, saved predictions and the scripts that rebuild every table and figure are available at \url{https://github.com/kayvinsoo7/scan-repair-foundation-models}.
